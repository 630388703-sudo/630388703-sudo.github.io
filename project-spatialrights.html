Project Overview

Spatial Rights is a VR spatial narrative that reveals the hidden labour infrastructure of public spaces — specifically, the invisible work zones of service workers inside a shopping mall. Built for an SDG Goal 8 course assignment, it evolved from 116 survey responses and field interviews with a cinema cleaner, school cleaner, and delivery worker into a complete interactive experience.

"The cleaner expands your space while erasing her own."

Research & Insights

❓
Design Question
How might we reveal invisible spatial inequality in public places — and make people feel its weight rather than just understand it intellectually?
Finding 1
Invisible Infrastructure

93% of surveyed visitors had never thought about where cleaning staff rest, eat, or store equipment. The infrastructure that maintains public comfort is designed to be invisible to its beneficiaries.

Finding 2
Spatial Compression

Workers' space contracts as consumer space expands. A cinema cleaner described working in spaces that disappear when the public enters — brief, tight windows between showtimes.

03

Three-Space VR Design

Space 1
The Block Room

Monument Valley-inspired. Player stacks blocks to build consumer amenities. Progression: child's playroom → crowded construction site → grey ruin. Consumption made literal.

Space 2
The Mirror Room

Identity-labelling space. Through NPC interactions, workers are systematically ignored or looked through. The player must watch — and cannot intervene. Discomfort as design.

Space 3
The Light-Dark Room

Cleaning expands light for consumers — and shrinks the worker's zone in real time. The player experiences directly how maintaining someone else's comfort compresses your own.

04

Physical Exhibition

Consumer Booth
3×3m

Spacious. Comfortable seating. Good lighting. Branded. The experience of the person being served.

Worker Booth
1×1.5m

Cramped. Minimal lighting. No seating. Unmarked. The experience of the person doing the serving.

💡
Design Intent
Booth assignment is random. The realisation that your experience of the same space is determined by which role you're assigned is the exhibition's first act of design.
05

Outcome & Reflection

Spatial Rights taught me spatial storytelling — that the most powerful narrative decisions aren't about dialogue, but about how you use space, movement and constraint to generate meaning. The two booths solved the problem of empathy at scale: you don't need to explain inequality when you can make people embody it.

  • 116-person survey + 3 field interviews as primary research
  • Designed three-space VR narrative with progressive perspective shift
  • Physical exhibition with spatially-asymmetric booths (3×3m vs 1×1.5m)
  • Built Blender mall/atrium environment for VR scenes
  • Developed full game script, storyboard and Game Flow table
